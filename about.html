An open atlas of oral-history and voice-testimony collections

Mapping Voices is an open digital-humanities research infrastructure that makes geographically and linguistically dispersed oral-history and voice-testimony collections discoverable through structured metadata, interactive mapping, and an openly licensed dataset.

The map is the interface. The research contribution is the documented, searchable, reusable metadata layer behind it.

01 Research question

Where are oral-history and voice-testimony collections located, which languages and communities do they represent, what themes and historical periods do they document, and how can this information be made more discoverable through an open digital research infrastructure?

Oral history is held by national sound archives, university folklore centres, UNESCO-recognised oral-tradition programmes, community memory projects, and testimony foundations. Each publishes its holdings on its own site, in its own language, with its own metadata. There is no shared, geographic, multilingual way to see what exists across them. Mapping Voices builds that common discovery layer and, just as importantly, makes the limits of that layer explicit.

02 Method

A collection is included when it is a real, publicly documented body of recorded speech, song, or testimony held by an identifiable institution or project, with a public page of its own that the record can link to. Secondary write-ups, private holdings, and anything that cannot be verified are excluded.

Countries, languages, and themes come from controlled vocabularies, so “USA”, “US”, and “United States” can never become three countries. Languages carry ISO 639-3 codes where the mapping is unambiguous; when a source names only a group (“multiple Kenyan languages”) the record says so rather than guessing individual languages. Language identity is never inferred from geography. Every record has a persistent identifier (MV-000123) and a verification status:

Verified
A curator has checked the source page and every core field against it.
Partially verified
The institution, link, and scope are confirmed; one or more fields are inferred or not yet checked field-by-field.
Needs review
A lead that has not yet cleared verification.
Source unavailable
A previously verified source that is no longer reachable. Kept, not deleted.

The full methodology — inclusion and exclusion criteria, geolocation rules, language and theme classification, duplicate handling, and corrections — is public: METHODOLOGY.md. Browse languages, their ISO codes, and the collections recorded in them in the Language Explorer, the thematic taxonomy in the Theme Explorer, and where the index does not yet reach in Coverage Gaps. Every field is defined in the data dictionary.

03 Open data

The dataset is licensed CC BY 4.0 and versioned. It describes and links to collections; it does not contain or redistribute their recordings.

  • collections.csvSpreadsheet-ready, one row per collection
  • collections.jsonCanonical records
  • collections.geojsonFor QGIS, ArcGIS, and web maps
  • languages.jsonLanguage vocabulary, ISO 639-3 codes, usage
  • themes.jsonTheme taxonomy and usage
  • countries.jsonCountries with ISO 3166 codes and UN M49 regions
  • stats.jsonAggregate counts for the indexed dataset
  • datapackage.jsonFrictionless Data Package descriptor

Filtered views of the atlas are addressable, e.g. ?country=Niger&language=Hausa, and each collection has a stable link via its identifier, e.g. ?c=MV-000023.

04 Ethics & representation

Mapping an archive does not grant permission to reproduce or redistribute its contents.

  • Link, don't host. Every record points to the holding institution's own page, and the institution's access conditions govern everything behind it.
  • Discoverability is not exposure. Testimony about genocide, incarceration, displacement, and violence is sensitive. Records describe collections at the level their custodians already publish, never individual testimonies or narrators.
  • Community and Indigenous ownership. Where a collection belongs to, or is governed by, a community, that governance takes precedence over this project's convenience. Custodians can ask for a record to be corrected or removed.
  • Naming. Institutional and language names follow the custodians' own usage where known; alternate names (including older or colonial exonyms) are recorded so they can be found, not so they can be preferred.
  • Pins are a choice. A pin marks where the voices come from when that differs from where the tapes are stored. That choice is documented per collection type in the methodology.

05 Technology

A deliberately small static stack: plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript with Leaflet and OpenStreetMap, no build step and no backend, deployed on GitHub Pages. The data are JSON files validated in continuous integration against the schema and controlled vocabularies, with a weekly link-rot check. The interface is available in English, Hausa, French, and Arabic (with right-to-left layout), is keyboard-operable, and treats the text index as a first-class alternative to the map. The technology stays subordinate to the research purpose.

06 Research applications

  • Locating testimony collections for a region, language, or theme before fieldwork or archival visits.
  • Comparing how languages and communities are represented across indexed archives.
  • Teaching archival research methods: verifying and contributing a real collection is a well-scoped exercise.
  • Reusing the dataset in GIS, bibliometric, or digital-humanities analyses, with citation.

07 Limitations

The atlas measures discoverable, indexed archival representation — not the existence or importance of oral traditions.

  • Coverage is uneven. Institutions with English-language web presences are easier to find and verify, which skews the dataset toward them.
  • The absence of a pin for a place or language is not evidence that no oral-history collection or tradition exists there.
  • Most records are currently partially verified: institutions and links are confirmed, but a field-by-field review against each source is still under way. In 19 records, languages are inferred from subject and place and flagged as such.
  • The decade_start/decade_end fields record when a collection was gathered, not the historical period its testimony discusses; the period discussed is recorded separately, so far only for the 40 collections principally about one bounded event, and derived from the record's own text for a curator to check (mappings).
  • Themes are discovery aids, not exhaustive descriptions of a collection.

08 Future directions

  1. v1.0 Research Atlas — field-by-field verification, historical-period metadata, archive type and access level.
  2. v1.5 Language Atlas — a Language Explorer, Glottolog and Wikidata identifiers, multilingual analysis.
  3. v2.0 Research Infrastructure — a read-only API and a reviewed contribution workflow.
  4. v2.5 Temporal Atlas — timeline views separating recording period from period discussed.
  5. v3.0 Collaborative Atlas — institutional and community contributions with attribution.

10 Contribute or correct

Institutions, researchers, and communities can propose a collection, correct a record, or ask for one to be changed or removed — no Git required. See CONTRIBUTING.md. Corrections are as valuable as new entries.

Map the archive. Preserve the context. Respect the source. Make the voices discoverable.
